\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter discusses the design, behaviour, and evaluation of 
MLguide in relation to the hypothesis and research questions stated in 
Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis-research-questions}. 
MLguide combines several components, an 
ontology of method knowledge, a semantic retrieval pipeline over 
articles, and generated starter notebooks. 
The discussion is therefore organised by topic 
and covers the method recommendations and the role of the articles, 
the coverage and structure of the ontology, 
the absence of production domain knowledge, the starter notebooks, the 
method feedback mechanism, the presentation of the recommendations, 
and the evaluation approach. Limitations are discussed under each 
topic. The chapter then returns to the hypothesis 
and research questions and answers each one based on the discussion. 
It ends with a section that draws the main limitations together and 
sets out the future work that follows from them.

\section{Method Recommendations and the Role of Articles}
\label{sec:method-recommendations-and-role-of-articles}

The method recommendations in MLguide are driven by the 
semantic similarity between the user's problem description and 
the titles and abstracts of articles in the database, as 
described in Section~\ref{sec:recommendation-process}. The 
articles retrieved are aggregated into method rankings, 
so the methods that appear in articles closely matching the 
problem are ranked highest. 

Across the evaluation, MLguide produced recommendations that 
were largely relevant to the problems submitted. In the user 
testing, most groups in Project 1 had high or medium overlap 
between the recommended methods and the methods they used, 
and several groups raised method shortlisting and early 
orientation as a benefit 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-1}). Groups in Project 2 reported 
that the recommended methods gave a useful overview of 
relevant approaches (Section~\ref{sec:project-2}). The 
example-based evaluation showed that most of the top-ranked 
methods were closely related to the problem in each case 
(Section~\ref{sec:results-example-based-evaluation}). At the 
same time, several observations point to systematic effects 
in how the recommendations are shaped, which are discussed 
further in this section.

The recommendations across the evaluation show a tendency to 
favour methods with a high article count, where article count 
is the number of articles that mention the method. This tendency 
is clearest for random forest, support vector machine, and neural 
network. These three methods are described in 
Section~\ref{sec:machine-learning-methods} as applicable to several 
tasks, including tasks in supervised learning. Supervised learning 
is reported in Section~\ref{sec:ml-in-product-development-and-production} 
as the most frequently used ML area in product development and 
production, and the same three methods are among the most frequently 
used there. This is also reflected in the article database, where 
supervised learning is the dominant area, as shown in 
Section~\ref{sec:resulting-database}. The dominance of these 
methods in the recommendations is therefore not surprising. The 
same methods appear prominently in the example-based 
evaluation. Neural network is the top recommendation in the 
reinforcement learning example with the highest article count, 
above several specific reinforcement learning methods.
Random forest is returned in the anomaly detection example 
despite not matching the task. In the user testing, neural 
network was recommended to several groups in both projects, 
although it was only used once. 

This effect has been pointed out for literature-based selection 
by \textcite{riesener2020}, who note that determining method 
relevance from the number of articles in the literature can put 
newer or less frequently reported methods at a disadvantage. 
MLguide does not rank methods strictly by article count, but 
methods that are mentioned in many articles are naturally more likely to 
appear among the top retrieved articles that drive the method 
ranking, so a similar effect is present. At the same time, 
methods with few articles are not excluded from the top of 
the list. In the supervised classification example shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:recommendations-example-1-rec}, Gaussian 
mixture model appears relatively high despite having only 33 
articles, against several thousand for random forest, support 
vector machine, and neural network. The same effect is visible in the 
anomaly detection example shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:recommendations-example-3-rec}, where local 
outlier factor is ranked second despite having only 48 articles. 
This shows that a method with few articles can still rank high 
if those articles match the problem description closely.

Another aspect of the article-based recommendation is that 
MLguide uses article titles and abstracts as the text input for 
retrieval, not full article content. The motivation for this 
choice is described in 
Section~\ref{sec:article-retrieval-and-knowledge-extraction}: 
abstracts provide a consistent representation of each article's 
core content, while full texts include sections that make them 
less comparable across a corpus, and storing and matching around 
17000 full texts would also be costly. Abstracts are also more 
comparable in length to a problem description, which suits the 
same sentence embedding model. However, several works reviewed in 
Section~\ref{sec:semantic-retrieval-from-scientific-literature} 
point to the limitations of this approach. 
\textcite{park2026chain_of_retrieval} report that relying only 
on abstracts is suboptimal because abstracts offer only sparse, 
high-level summaries of a paper, and 
\textcite{ostendorff2022_aspect_based_embeddings} find that 
embeddings of titles and abstracts capture the task and dataset of a 
paper well but its method poorly, because methodological detail 
is mentioned only marginally in titles and abstracts.

The summarising nature of abstracts has direct consequences for 
MLguide. A single abstract can mention several methods from one 
study, for example one that performed best and others that were 
tested as baselines. These different roles are not reflected in 
the ranking produced by MLguide. All mentioned methods are treated as 
relevant to the problem the article addresses. The same applies 
when a method is used as part of a pipeline rather than as the 
main contribution, such as a dimensionality reduction step 
applied before the main method. Principal component analysis, 
described in Section~\ref{sec:machine-learning-methods} as a 
dimensionality reduction method, appears among the 
recommendations in the supervised classification example 
(Section~\ref{sec:example-1}), likely for this reason. The same 
issue applies to the classification of articles into ML areas, 
which is based on keywords in titles and abstracts. An article 
that presents an unsupervised approach while contrasting it 
with a supervised baseline can match both ML areas. As a 
result, methods that are mentioned but not recommended by the 
original authors, that are used as preprocessing steps, or 
that belong to a different ML area than the article's main 
contribution, can still appear in the recommendations.


The findings in this thesis are in line with the observation 
made by \textcite{ostendorff2022_aspect_based_embeddings}
that task and dataset are captured better than method details in abstracts. 
Across the three example-based evaluations in 
Section~\ref{sec:results-example-based-evaluation}, the 
top-ranked articles for the recommended methods are generally 
relevant to the problem at hand. In the user testing, three 
groups in Project 2 also reported that MLguide was useful for 
finding or confirming relevant articles 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-2}). A user's problem description can also
be expected to describe the task and the data more than the method, 
since the method is what the user is looking for. 
The retrieval therefore appears to work well on 
the topical side of the problem, while methodological match 
is harder to assess from the matching alone.

A further challenge in extracting method names from text is handling 
cases where one method name is contained in another, such as neural 
network within recurrent neural network. Each such case has to be 
handled explicitly. The article retrieval pipeline described in 
Section~\ref{sec:article-retrieval-and-knowledge-extraction} handles 
this for neural network, but the results suggest that not all relevant 
cases are covered. In the anomaly detection example, support vector 
machine is at the top of the recommendations, while one-class SVM 
appears further down. One-class SVM is described in 
Section~\ref{sec:machine-learning-methods} as an anomaly detection 
method, and the top-ranked articles for support vector machine in this 
example include an article that explicitly concerns a one-class model. 
This indicates that articles about one-class SVM contribute to the 
article count of both methods, but support vector machine benefits 
from its much larger overall article count and ranks higher as a 
result.

The effect is not only on the ranking. The articles shown as the basis 
for support vector machine then include work that in fact concerns 
one-class SVM, so the justification presented to the user is 
misleading. The explicit handling could be extended to more such 
cases, but each case has to be identified and added by hand, which 
scales poorly as the method set grows. A more general approach would 
be to recognise method names in a way that accounts for one name being 
contained in another, rather than treating each overlap separately.

Another point is that literature-based recommendations reflect 
the methods that appear in published research, which may differ from 
what is commonly used in practice or taught in ML courses. In the 
user testing for the anomaly detection problem in Project 1, several 
groups reported that neural network methods were over-represented in 
the recommendations and that Gaussian mixture model and one-class 
SVM were missing (Section~\ref{sec:project-1}). Gaussian mixture model 
and one-class SVM are classical methods for 
anomaly detection, whereas neural network methods have a broader 
range of applications 
(Sections~\ref{sec:machine-learning-methods} 
and~\ref{sec:neural-networks-and-deep-learning}). This broad 
applicability is part of why neural network methods have a high 
article count, which, as noted above, favours them across many 
problems. It is also reasonable to assume that research literature tends to 
use more advanced models than the typical starting points for users 
new to machine learning, so recommendations grounded in research can 
differ from what students or beginners expect from their course 
material.

The supervised classification example in 
Section~\ref{sec:example-1} shows a different balance, where 
classical methods such as linear regression and k-nearest neighbours 
also have high article counts. Simpler classical methods are therefore not 
under-represented as a whole, but they can be under-represented 
within specific tasks or areas.

In the supervised classification example 
(Section~\ref{sec:example-1}), Gaussian mixture model is ranked 
second despite being flagged as not suited for the classification 
task. A likely reason is that the wording of the problem 
description, which includes terms such as "faulty", resembles how an 
unsupervised anomaly detection problem might be described. This 
wording matches articles in which Gaussian mixture model is used for 
anomaly detection, and the resulting article-based score is high 
enough to place it as the second recommendation. The case shows that 
relying on the problem description alone, without using the ontology 
to filter on attributes such as the selected task, can rank a method 
that the system has itself flagged as unsuited for that task.

\section{Ontology Coverage and Structure}
\label{sec:ontology-coverage-and-structure}

The ontology in MLguide structures the method knowledge that 
complements the unstructured knowledge spread across the article 
database. It supports the filters in the user interface, gives 
transparency in how the recommendations are presented, and is 
designed to be extended over time. The hierarchy lets related 
methods share properties through inheritance, as described in 
Section~\ref{sec:ml-and-ontology}, which makes it easier to add 
new methods without specifying every property from scratch. 
This makes the ontology more flexible than a flat matrix as used 
by \textcite{riesener2020} and 
\textcite{lickert2021_ml_selection_reverse_logistics}, and more 
formal than the semantic wiki of 
\textcite{gerschutz2021semantic_web_approach}. It also supports 
the kind of transparency that AutoML approaches are often 
criticised for lacking 
\parencite{barbudo2023eight_years_automl_categorisation_review_trends}. 
The ontology-based approach is comparable to AI4PD 
\parencite{gerschutz2023_AI4PD}, which also uses an ontology, 
there to link data-driven methods to product development processes.

The usefulness of the ontology depends on how completely its 
properties are populated. In Project 1, several groups reported 
that the performance preferences were not useful in practice 
and that the task match was incorrect for some methods 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-1}). This was system version A, and 
the findings led to a temporary removal of these filters in 
version B while the method knowledge was being extended, as 
described in Section~\ref{sec:changes-across-system-versions}. 
Version C, used in the example-based evaluation, has higher 
property coverage. Most methods now have task and dataset type relations, 
and many have performance preferences defined, while the note properties, 
such as conditions for when a method is possible or not 
recommended, are still sparsely populated. The overall coverage 
is not complete, as shown in 
Table~\ref{tab:ml-approach-coverage-extended}.

The incomplete coverage reflects a wider challenge with 
ontology-based method knowledge. Populating the properties 
manually is time-consuming, and it is difficult to keep up with 
a growing set of methods and tasks. Related work acknowledges 
this. \textcite{gerschutz2023_AI4PD} report that populating 
their ontology with method knowledge is done manually and that 
the effort required is high. \textcite{sonntag2023pattern_ml_product_development} 
and \textcite{lickert2021_ml_selection_reverse_logistics} 
restrict their work to a much smaller number of methods, which keeps 
the effort manageable but limits the scope. MLguide aims to 
cover a broader range of methods across the full product 
lifecycle, which is more ambitious in scope but exposes the 
manual-effort limitation more clearly.

Some structural choices in the ontology are also worth discussing. 
The dataset type of a method is not stored as a property on the 
method itself, but is reached through the \texttt{used\_for} relation 
to a task, as explained in 
Section~\ref{sec:ml-ontology-structure-and-content}. A method 
therefore matches a dataset type only if it is connected to a task 
that has that dataset type defined. One result is that the same 
information can be expressed in two ways, a task such as "tabular 
regression" overlaps with the combination of the task "regression" 
and a tabular dataset type. Another result is that whether a method 
matches a dataset type depends entirely on the tasks it happens to be 
linked to. In the 
supervised classification example (Section~\ref{sec:example-1}), 
principal component analysis is not marked as matching the tabular 
dataset type, because the tasks PCA is linked to in the ontology do 
not have tabular defined as a dataset type. Storing dataset type as 
its own property on the method would avoid such cases and make the 
ontology easier to query and reason over. It would also follow the 
principle that an ontology should be designed so that it can be 
extended without changing existing definitions, as described in 
Section~\ref{sec:ontology-description} 
\parencite{gruber1995toward_principles_design_ontologies_used_knowledge_sharing}, 
since dataset type could then be adjusted or extended on its own, 
without changing how methods and tasks are already linked.

The structure is more flexible in how methods relate to ML 
areas. As shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:ontology-uml-with-extension}, methods are not 
linked directly to ML areas. Instead, they are linked to tasks 
through \texttt{used\_for}, and tasks belong to ML areas. A 
method is also linked to the articles that mention it, and 
each article is classified by ML area, so a method can be 
associated with multiple areas through both tasks and 
articles. A method that does not fit neatly into the three-way 
division of supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement 
learning can therefore still be part of the ontology and 
appear in recommendations. Example 2 
(Section~\ref{sec:example-2}) illustrates this. Two 
metaheuristic methods appear in the recommendations for a 
reinforcement learning problem, genetic algorithm at third 
place and particle swarm optimization further down. Neither 
is a reinforcement learning method, but the retrieved 
articles connect them to similar scheduling problems. This is 
consistent with the literature, where evolutionary algorithms 
are often combined with the three common ML areas 
(Section~\ref{sec:machine-learning-paradigms-and-tasks}). The 
ontology accommodates this without forcing a classification 
that would not be a good fit.

The ML Ontology developed by \textcite{humm2026industry_ready_machine_learning_ontology} 
was originally built to support AutoML rather 
than method selection. As a result, several of its classes are 
not used in MLguide, and a number of extensions had to be added 
to support the article-method links and the filters that 
MLguide depends on. Building on an existing ontology gives 
MLguide a stable foundation and aligns it with a broader effort 
in the field, but it also means that the structure was not 
designed for the exact purpose at hand.
This reflects the trade-off in ontology design described in 
Section~\ref{sec:ontology-description}, where a more reusable and 
general ontology tends to be less directly usable for a specific 
task and requires refinement before it fits 
\parencite{studer1998knowledge_engineering_principles_methods}. 
The design nevertheless leaves room for MLguide to be 
extended with AutoML knowledge in the future, similar to 
how OMA-ML 
\parencite{zender2022ontology_based_meta_automl} builds on 
the same ML Ontology to support AutoML 
(Section~\ref{sec:tools-and-platforms}).

The link between the ontology and the articles is one of the 
strengths of MLguide compared with the structured frameworks presented
in Section~\ref{sec:approaches-to-method-selection}, which rely 
entirely on manually populated method knowledge. In MLguide, 
each article is automatically classified by ML area and linked 
to the methods it mentions, as part of the article retrieval 
pipeline 
(Section~\ref{sec:article-retrieval-and-knowledge-extraction}). 
This means the article-method connection grows as the article 
base grows, without manual effort for each method. 
Other ontology-based work reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related-work-and-existing-tools} 
also makes use of links from the 
ontology to articles. NLP-KG \parencite{schopf2024nlp} links 
publications to fields of study and uses the graph for 
exploratory search, while ITO 
\parencite{blagec2022_benchmark_knowledge_graph} imports 
articles automatically from Papers with Code and links them 
to benchmark results for provenance. These approaches show 
that more can be extracted from the literature than what 
MLguide currently uses, and that automated import can reduce 
the manual effort required to keep an ontology up to date.

A natural direction for further work is therefore to extend 
the article retrieval pipeline to bring more structured 
knowledge into the ontology automatically. Possible additions 
include benchmark results, task or application area 
information, and combinations of methods used together in a 
pipeline or hybrid architecture. 
The last point connects to two observations made earlier. 
Section~\ref{sec:method-recommendations-and-role-of-articles} 
discussed how methods used in a pipeline, such as 
dimensionality reduction before a main method, can appear in 
the recommendations because the retrieval does not distinguish 
between them. Project 2 reports also noted that MLguide is 
limited to single-algorithm recommendations and does not 
capture hybrid architectures 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-2}). Extracting method combinations 
from the literature could turn this limitation into a feature, 
by treating pipelines and hybrid architectures as recommendable 
units in themselves. These extensions 
would further reduce the manual effort required to keep the ontology 
up to date, and build on the article-ontology link that MLguide 
already has.

\section{Production Domain Knowledge}
\label{sec:production-domain-knowledge}

The previous section discussed how the article retrieval 
pipeline could be extended to bring more structured knowledge 
into the ontology automatically. One area where this would 
matter is the representation of production domain knowledge in 
the recommendations, which is the focus of this section.

The ontology in MLguide represents general machine learning 
knowledge, organised around ML areas, tasks, dataset types, 
and performance preferences. The ontology does not represent production domain knowledge, 
which is the kinds of problems found in product development and 
production, such as predictive maintenance, quality 
prediction, or fault diagnosis 
(Section~\ref{sec:applications-of-machine-learning}). The two 
dimensions from the pre-master project that would have brought 
such knowledge into the system, product lifecycle phase and 
application area, are not used in MLguide. As described in 
Section~\ref{sec:adaptations-for-mlguide}, both were dropped 
because the classification of articles along these dimensions 
was not reliable enough to support filtering without risking 
the exclusion of relevant articles.
Bringing such knowledge back into the 
system is the kind of extension discussed in the previous section, 
where more structured knowledge would be drawn from the articles 
automatically. This would require a more reliable classification than 
the one used in the pre-master project, built on clearer categories 
that articles can be assigned to with greater confidence.

The consequence of not having production domain knowledge in the ontology
is that the production-domain orientation of 
the recommendations comes only from the article retrieval. 
The semantic similarity between 
the user's problem description and the article abstracts 
pulls in articles that are topically related to the problem, 
including its application context, even though no explicit 
representation links methods to production domains. This works 
in part, but the system has no way of guaranteeing that a 
recommendation is appropriate for a specific application area.

Some of the frameworks reviewed in 
Section~\ref{sec:approaches-to-method-selection} take a narrower 
approach to scope.
\textcite{lickert2021_ml_selection_reverse_logistics} works 
within reverse logistics, and 
\textcite{sonntag2023pattern_ml_product_development} and \textcite{sonntag2024_mcda_framework} 
focus on early product development. The scope makes it 
feasible to characterise methods against a relevant set of 
problems for that domain, but limits how widely the framework 
can be applied. MLguide makes the opposite trade-off, broad 
coverage across the full product lifecycle, but no domain 
knowledge built into the ontology yet. A possible improvement is 
therefore to link methods to 
domain-specific problems in the ontology and to filter or weight the 
articles by application area. This would keep the broad coverage of 
MLguide while adding the domain grounding of the narrower frameworks.

The reason this is left as future work is again the manual 
effort. Characterising every method against a set of 
production-domain problems is a substantial task, and the 
article classification would have to be accurate enough to 
support filtering. If domain knowledge is added but left 
incomplete, partial coverage may give worse results than no 
filter at all, because methods that should match a domain 
would appear unmarked.

Without explicit filters for the production domain, the way 
the user describes the problem becomes the main signal for what 
the system should retrieve. MLguide uses free-text problem 
descriptions as the main input, supported by an optional filter 
for ML area.
The pattern language of 
\textcite{sonntag2023pattern_ml_product_development}, by 
contrast, requires the user to formalise the entire problem 
in a fixed set of attributes before matching. In their user 
study the correct method was identified in 64\% of 
cases, and the errors arose mainly from incorrect manual 
formalisation rather than from the matching itself. This 
suggests that requiring users to fill in fixed attributes to 
scope their problem can introduce errors. The free-text 
approach in MLguide keeps the burden of formalisation away 
from the user and lets the system interpret the description 
through semantic similarity. The anomaly detection example in 
Section~\ref{sec:example-3} shows that this can work without 
the ML area filter applied. With only a free-text problem 
description and a task selection, the top recommendations 
were still relevant anomaly detection methods. This indicates 
that the system is usable without multiple filters
for scoping the problem.

An interesting and potentially positive effect of not having 
production domain knowledge in the ontology is that MLguide is 
relatively easy to adapt to a different domain. The article base can 
be replaced with articles from another field, and the recommendations 
will reflect that field through the semantic retrieval. This 
flexibility comes from the same design choice that limits the 
domain-specific precision. Adding deeper production domain knowledge 
would therefore be a trade-off. It could improve precision within 
product development and production, but would make the system less 
flexible and portable. This reflects the same trade-off in ontology 
design as discussed in the previous section, where 
a more general ontology is more reusable but less directly suited to a 
specific task.

\section{Starter Notebooks and Implementation Support}
\label{sec:starter-notebooks-and-implementation-support}

Starter notebooks are one of three output types in MLguide. 
They give the user a concrete starting point for implementing 
a chosen method. Two groups in Project 1 reported code 
templates and implementation hints as a benefit 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-1}). The evidence base is limited, 
but the observation indicates that the output type has a place 
in how the system is used.

A notebook is positioned as a starting point for the user, not 
a replacement for implementation. This is different from 
AutoML systems, which produce a trained model directly and are often 
described as treating the selection and configuration process as a black box
(Section~\ref{sec:approaches-to-method-selection}). MLguide 
leaves the implementation to the user but provides a 
structured starting point that tries to lower the barrier to begin. 
This is also a more direct combination of method selection and 
implementation support than what the reviewed work offers. The 
frameworks for method selection in 
Section~\ref{sec:approaches-to-method-selection} stop at the 
method recommendation. Traingenerator 
\parencite{rieke2021traingenerator} generates code templates 
but assumes that the method has already been chosen. MLguide 
combines the two, a recommended method and a notebook that 
gives a starting point for implementing it.

The current templates are simple, and the coverage is 
incomplete. Adding 
a template for each method is manual work, similar to the 
manual effort that limits the ontology coverage. However, the 
technical structure is straightforward, and a new 
template only needs to be placed in the right folder with the right
method file name
to be picked up by the system, which makes it easy to extend over 
time.

Reinforcement learning methods are not covered by any 
templates, as noted in 
Section~\ref{sec:template-structure-and-extensibility}. This 
showed in Example 2, where Q-learning was recommended but no 
template was available (Section~\ref{sec:example-2}). 
Supporting reinforcement learning would require a different 
approach. Options include providing templates for common 
environment patterns, or leaving the environment definition 
to the user while supplying a skeleton for the agent and the 
training loop. Investigating these options is left as future 
work.

The templates could also be developed further beyond what 
they contain today. The user could 
be given options to choose or adjust parameters through the 
interface before the notebook is generated, similar to the 
sidebar interface in Traingenerator described in 
Section~\ref{sec:tools-and-platforms}. However, parameter selection 
can be difficult for users new to machine learning, 
which is part of the target group for MLguide. A default 
configuration should therefore remain available so that the 
notebook works as a starting point even without further 
input from the user. The underlying ontology already supports 
this kind of extension. The \texttt{configuration\_item} 
class from the ML Ontology, shown in 
Section~\ref{sec:ml-ontology-structure-and-content}, gives a 
structure for storing parameters and their values. Filling 
this with reasonable defaults, possibly extracted from 
articles where parameter choices are reported, would let 
the system suggest defaults that go beyond a fixed template 
while remaining useful to users that do not want to choose 
themselves.

\section{Method Feedback}
\label{sec:method-feedback}

The method-specific feedback mechanism, given through the 
rating widget on the method details page 
(Section~\ref{sec:ui-method-details-page}), was introduced 
in version C and was therefore not part of the user testing 
in Project 1 or Project 2 
(Section~\ref{sec:changes-across-system-versions}). Few 
ratings have been submitted so far, and the mechanism could 
not be evaluated as a data source. This is the cold start 
problem presented in 
Section~\ref{sec:rating-based-user-feedback},
ratings only become useful when enough of them have been 
collected. The Bayesian smoothing in the feedback boost 
(Section~\ref{sec:feedback-boost}) is designed to handle a 
small user base by pulling the estimate towards a neutral 
prior when ratings are sparse. The combined ranking still 
relies mostly on the similarity-based score. This is 
a deliberate design choice for the current stage, but it 
also means that feedback is unlikely to influence the 
recommendations meaningfully until the user base grows.

Each rating is stored together with the full problem 
description and the rated method, as described in 
Section~\ref{sec:database-structure}. This structure 
supports content-based filtering 
\parencite[Chapter~4]{ricci2010recommender_systems_handbook}, 
where recommendations are based on similarity between items 
that the users have rated before. Since each rating 
in MLguide is tied to the problem description it was given 
for, the similarity between a user's current problem and 
problems that have been rated before could feed into the 
ranking, giving more weight to methods that have been rated 
highly for similar problems. In this way the system would 
collect knowledge from 
its users and use it directly in the recommendations, which 
would reduce some of the manual-effort limitations discussed 
in earlier sections by drawing on user input rather than only 
on updates to the data sources. 

\section{Presentation of the Recommendations}
\label{sec:presentation-of-the-recommendations}

How the recommendations are presented affects how they are read.
This was not a main focus of the thesis, but the results suggest 
it matters more than expected. Groups in both Project 1 and 
Project 2 reported that the ranking was unclear and that it was 
hard to tell which method to choose 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-1}, Section~\ref{sec:project-2}). The 
methods are in fact listed in ranked order in the recommendation 
table (Figure~\ref{fig:user-interface-recommendation-table}), 
but the table is not numbered and gives only a weak visual 
cue that the order carries meaning.

The number of methods shown adds to this. Ten methods are returned, 
which is a high number for a user who wants a clear starting point. A 
long list without a visible ranking gives weak guidance on where to 
begin and makes the output harder to act on, which works against the 
goal of supporting users who are new to machine learning. Presenting 
fewer methods, numbering them, or otherwise making the ranking 
visible would likely make the recommendations clearer. 
The reviewed frameworks present fewer methods as recommended. 
They work from small, manually curated sets, from two methods in 
the pattern language of 
\textcite{sonntag2023pattern_ml_product_development} to eleven 
in \textcite{riesener2020} and sixteen in the MCDA framework of 
\textcite{sonntag2024_mcda_framework}, and they narrow the user 
towards a single method through best match, elimination, or a 
ranking with a clear winner. MLguide instead draws on the full 
set of 187 methods in the ontology and returns ten,
a natural consequence of its broader approach. MLguide also labels 
the output as "Possible ML methods", which 
is technically correct but does not tell the user how to read 
the list. 
Showing several candidates rather than pointing to a 
single method is a reasonable choice when no single recommendation is certain,
but as the feedback suggests, 
the presentation should make it clearer that the list is a set of 
ranked candidate methods.

\section{Evaluation Approach}
\label{sec:evaluation-approach}

The two evaluation methods used for MLguide are complementary, 
but each has limitations that shape how the results should be 
read.

The user testing was carried out with engineering students 
in a machine learning course, which matches the intended 
user group of MLguide described in 
Section~\ref{sec:design-goals}: engineers and students new 
to machine learning. The two course projects had different 
topics and gave the students real implementation tasks to 
carry out (Section~\ref{sec:user-testing}). The students 
also chose their own specific problems within the project 
briefs, so the system was tested on a range of different 
problems rather than on repeated variations of the same one.

The user testing also has some constraints worth noting. 
The two projects used different system versions, 
as shown in the development timeline in 
Section~\ref{sec:development-timeline}. This reflects the 
iterative nature of the development, but it means that 
findings from Project 1 and Project 2 are not directly 
comparable. Neither project tested the final version C, which 
was the version used in the example-based evaluation. 
The number of groups using MLguide was also lower in Project 
2 than in Project 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:participation-count-p2}). 
Project 2 had a more open-ended and research-oriented 
task brief, while Project 1 
asked the groups to explore a more defined problem. The 
defined-problem setup matches better with the recommendation 
process MLguide is designed around, where the user has a 
specific problem and looks for a method to use. This explains 
why Project 1 generated more direct feedback on the method 
recommendations, while Project 2 generated more feedback on 
the article retrieval.

None of the groups used the structured feedback 
form available in the system, so all feedback came as free text in the 
project reports without a fixed format. Interpreting the 
reports involved subjective judgement on the part of the 
author. A likely reason the feedback form was not used is 
that it was an optional feature in MLguide, not a required 
part of the course deliverable.

The groups were also free to use MLguide at any point in 
their project. In Project 1, about half the groups used the 
system early, before choosing methods, and about half used 
it after the methods had already been chosen 
(Figure~\ref{fig:timing-p1}). The overlap between 
recommendations and used methods was similar for both early and late groups 
(Figure~\ref{fig:overlap-by-timing-p1}), but the overlap 
means different things in the two cases. For groups that 
used MLguide early, the overlap indicates whether the system 
influenced their choice of methods. For groups that used it 
late, the overlap only indicates whether the recommendations 
match methods that had already been chosen on a different 
basis, likely from course material or online resources. 
Several groups recognised this themselves, and the most 
commonly reported issue in Project 1 was that MLguide was 
used too late to influence method decisions 
(Figure~\ref{fig:issues-p1}).

The free use of the system also highlights an unintended 
use case. Several groups that used MLguide late reported 
that they used it to confirm or validate methods they had 
already chosen (Figure~\ref{fig:benefits-p1}). MLguide 
therefore appears to serve two roles, helping users choose 
methods when used early, and providing a check on methods 
chosen elsewhere when used late.

The example-based evaluation complements the user testing by 
offering controlled input. This made it possible to examine 
the recommendation behaviour in detail and to detect 
patterns that would have been difficult to identify from open use 
alone, such as the high occurrence of methods with high article counts.
The trade-off is that the examples were constructed 
by the author, and the output was interpreted by the author 
as well. This introduces a risk of bias in both how the 
problem descriptions were phrased and how the results were 
read. The number of examples is also small and covers only a 
limited part of the range of problems MLguide could be 
applied to.

The two evaluations together give a reasonable basis for discussing how the 
system behaves and where its limitations lie, but the findings should be 
understood within the scope of this evaluation. A larger and more independent 
evaluation would give stronger conclusions. Because MLguide is an open web 
application, there is in principle no restriction on who can use it. This makes 
it possible to evaluate the system far more broadly than was done here. The 
course-based setting could be extended to several machine learning courses, of 
different types and at different universities. The evaluation could also reach 
beyond students towards the other part of the intended user group, engineers in 
industry who are not machine learning experts. The feedback form built into the 
home page makes it possible to collect feedback data continuously, from anyone who 
uses the system.

\section{Answers to Research Questions}
\label{sec:answers-to-research-questions}

MLguide is the artifact developed in this thesis. Its 
development was guided by the design goals in 
Section~\ref{sec:design-goals}, and the system was built to 
investigate the hypothesis and research questions stated in 
Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis-research-questions}. This section 
returns to each research question and answers it based on the 
findings and reflections in this chapter.

\subsection{RQ1: Ontology-Based Knowledge Representation}

The first research question asks how ontology-based knowledge 
representation can support the recommendation of machine 
learning methods.

In MLguide, the ontology gives structure to the method knowledge that 
complements the unstructured knowledge in the article database. It 
grounds the recommendation by supplying the candidate methods, 
retrieved from the knowledge graph for the selected ML area, where a 
method is only included if at least one article mentions it. The main 
strength of the ontology is therefore this link to the articles. Each 
article is classified by ML area and linked to the methods it 
mentions, so the method knowledge grows as the article base grows, 
without manual work for each method. This sets MLguide apart from 
method-selection frameworks that rely on a fixed, manually built table 
of methods. The ontology also provides the filters in the user 
interface and presents structured information about each method, such 
as its task and dataset type, alongside the recommendation. Related 
methods share properties through inheritance, so new methods can be 
added without defining every property from scratch.

The support is limited by how completely the ontology is populated. 
Several properties have low coverage, and filling them by hand is 
slow, which the broad scope of MLguide makes more visible than work 
that stays within a smaller domain. The low coverage is also the main 
reason the ontology contributes little to the ranking itself at 
present, since attributes that are only partly populated cannot be 
used to filter or rank without risking the exclusion of valid 
candidates. The ontology represents general machine learning knowledge and does not 
capture the kinds of problems found in product development and 
production, so the orientation towards that domain comes from the 
article retrieval rather than from the structured representation. The 
article-method link that already fills part of the ontology 
automatically could be extended to bring in more structured knowledge 
from the literature, including domain information, which would reduce 
the manual effort and let the ontology take on a larger part of the 
ranking. One structural choice also affects the recommendations, 
reaching dataset type through the task rather than storing it on the 
method, which can cause valid matches to be missed.

\subsection{RQ2: Semantic Retrieval from Scientific Literature}

The second research question asks how semantic retrieval from 
scientific literature can support and justify the recommendation 
of machine learning methods.

Semantic retrieval ranks the articles by their relevance to the 
problem description, and the method recommendations are built by 
aggregating the article ranks into a score for each method they 
mention. Across the evaluation and user testing, this produced 
recommendations that were largely relevant to the problems 
submitted. The recommendations favour methods that appear 
frequently in the literature, which tend to be general methods 
that work across many problems, but more specific methods still 
surface when their articles match the problem closely. A method 
with few articles can rank high on a strong topical match, as 
Gaussian mixture model and local outlier factor did in the examples, 
so the retrieval does not simply reproduce the most common methods. 
The problem description alone is enough to produce a reasonable 
result, as the anomaly detection example showed without the ML area 
filter applied. Because the recommendations 
are carried by the articles, they can also be moved to a new 
domain by collecting articles from that field, without changing 
the structured knowledge.

The articles also serve as the justification for a 
recommendation. They are shown alongside each method, so the 
user can see the basis for the method directly. This justification is 
only as strong as what the articles say about the method, which 
the system does not check. A method counts as supported whenever 
a matching article mentions it, even when the article uses it 
only as a baseline or as one step in a pipeline. Principal 
component analysis appears in the supervised classification 
example for this reason, since it is picked up from articles 
that use it as a preprocessing step rather than as the main 
method. A second weakness is that the extraction does not separate closely 
related method names. In the anomaly detection example, articles about 
one-class SVM raised the score of standard support vector machine, so 
some of the articles shown for it in fact described a different 
method. 

\subsection{RQ3: Support for Users in Early Implementation}

The third research question asks to what extent MLguide can 
support users with limited machine learning expertise in the 
early stages of implementation.

The support takes the form of a starter notebook generated for 
the recommended method. It gives the user a concrete starting 
point, but only that, the implementation itself is left to the 
user. This is a more direct link between method selection and 
implementation than the reviewed frameworks offer, which stop at 
the recommendation, but it is a modest form of support. Two 
groups in Project 1 reported the code templates as useful, which 
is some evidence that the output type has a place, though too 
little to draw firm conclusions from.

The support is also uneven. The templates are simple and cover 
only part of the method set, and reinforcement learning methods 
have no templates at all, as the missing notebook for Q-learning 
in Example 2 showed. Adding templates is manual work, much like 
populating the ontology, but the structure makes it easy to 
extend, since a new template only has to be placed in the right 
folder. In its current form, MLguide supports early 
implementation in a limited but concrete way, with clear room to 
extend both the coverage and the depth of the notebooks.

\subsection{Hypothesis}

Taken together, the three research questions address the hypothesis 
that a system combining ontology-based knowledge representation, 
semantic similarity to scientific literature, and structured 
implementation support can provide relevant and justified method 
recommendations for users with limited machine learning expertise, 
and support the first steps of implementing the chosen recommended method. The 
findings support this in part. MLguide combines all three elements in 
a working system, and the recommendations were largely relevant to the 
problems submitted. The ontology contributes less than it could, 
because several of its properties have low coverage and cannot yet be 
used to shape the ranking. Most of the work is therefore carried by 
the semantic retrieval, which ranks methods through the articles that 
mention them. The weaker part is justification. A method counts as 
supported whenever a matching article mentions it, even when the 
article uses it only as a baseline or as one step in a pipeline, so a 
recommendation is not always justified as well as it appears. 
The implementation support is real but limited, since the notebooks 
remain simple and do not yet cover the full method set. 
The hypothesis therefore holds on three counts: relevance, the first 
steps of implementation, and the combination of the three elements, 
with justification as the main reservation and the scale of the 
evaluation as a further limit.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations-and-future-work}

The main limitations identified across this chapter are summarised 
below, together with the directions for future work that follows from each.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Several properties in the ontology have low coverage, and 
    populating them by hand is slow, which the broad scope of MLguide 
    makes more visible 
    (Section~\ref{sec:ontology-coverage-and-structure}). Production 
    domain knowledge is not represented, so the orientation towards 
    product development and production is provided only by the article 
    retrieval (Section~\ref{sec:production-domain-knowledge}). In 
    addition to populating the ontology manually, or as an alternative 
    to it, the article-method link that already fills part of the 
    ontology automatically could be extended to draw more structured 
    knowledge from the literature, including domain information, 
    benchmark results, and method combinations, which would reduce the 
    manual effort and allow the ontology to contribute more to the 
    ranking of methods.

    \item A method is counted as supported whenever a matching article 
    mentions it, even when the article uses it as a baseline or as a 
    step in a pipeline, and the extraction does not separate all cases of 
    method names that are contained in other names
    (Section~\ref{sec:method-recommendations-and-role-of-articles}). 
    The extraction could be extended to account for the role a method 
    plays in an article and to recognise names contained in other 
    names, so that the articles shown as justification describe the 
    recommended method more accurately.

    \item The notebook templates are simple and cover only part of the 
    method set, and reinforcement learning methods have no templates at all
    (Section~\ref{sec:starter-notebooks-and-implementation-support}). 
    More templates should be added, a separate approach could be 
    developed for reinforcement learning, and configurable parameters 
    with sensible defaults could be offered through the 
    \texttt{configuration\_item} structure already present in the 
    ontology.

    \item Few ratings have been submitted, so the feedback mechanism 
    could not be evaluated and contributes little to the ranking at 
    present (Section~\ref{sec:method-feedback}). Once enough ratings 
    have been collected, the problem description stored with each 
    rating could feed into the ranking through content-based 
    filtering, giving more weight to methods rated highly for similar 
    problems.

    \item Two system versions were used across the two course projects, 
    neither of which tested the final version, and the example-based 
    evaluation was small and interpreted by the author, which limits 
    how far the findings can be generalised 
    (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-approach}). A larger and more 
    independent evaluation, extended across several machine learning 
    courses and towards engineers in industry, would give stronger 
    conclusions.
\end{itemize}
