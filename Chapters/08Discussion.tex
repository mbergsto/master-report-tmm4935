\section{Method Recommendations and the Role of Articles}
\label{sec:method-recommendations-and-role-of-articles}

The method recommendations in MLguide are driven by the 
semantic similarity between the user's problem description and 
the titles and abstracts of articles in the database, as 
described in Section~\ref{sec:recommendation-process}. The 
articles retrieved are aggregated into method rankings, 
so the methods that appear in articles closely matching the 
problem are ranked highest. 

Across the evaluation, MLguide produced recommendations that 
were largely relevant to the problems submitted. In the user 
testing, most groups in Project 1 had high or medium overlap 
between the recommended methods and the methods they used, 
and several groups raised method shortlisting and early 
orientation as a benefit 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-1}). Groups in Project 2 reported 
that the recommended methods gave a useful overview of 
relevant approaches (Section~\ref{sec:project-2}). The 
example-based evaluation showed that most of the top-ranked 
methods were closely related to the problem in each case 
(Section~\ref{sec:results-example-based-evaluation}). At the 
same time, several observations point to systematic effects 
in how the recommendations are shaped, which are discussed in 
the rest of this section.

The recommendations across the evaluation show a tendency to 
favour methods with a high article count, in particular Random 
Forest, Support Vector Machine, and Neural Network. These three 
methods are described in 
Section~\ref{sec:machine-learning-methods} as applicable to 
several tasks, including tasks in supervised learning, which 
Section~\ref{sec:ml-in-product-development-and-production} 
reports as the most frequently used ML area in product 
development and production, with Random Forest, Support Vector 
Machine, and Neural Network among the most frequently used 
methods. This is also reflected in the article database, where 
supervised learning is the dominant area, as shown in 
Section~\ref{sec:resulting-database}. The dominance of these 
methods in the recommendations is therefore not surprising. The 
same methods appear prominently in the example-based 
evaluation. Neural Network is the top recommendation in the 
reinforcement learning example with the highest article count, 
above several specific Reinforcement Learning based methods.
Random Forest is returned in the anomaly detection example 
despite not matching the task. In the user testing, Neural 
Network was recommended to several groups in both projects, 
although it was only used once. 

This effect has been pointed out for literature-based selection 
by \textcite{riesener2020}, who note that determining method 
relevance from the number of articles in the literature can put 
newer or less frequently reported methods at a disadvantage. 
MLguide does not rank methods strictly by article count, but 
methods that are mentioned in many articles are more likely to 
appear among the top retrieved articles that drive the method 
ranking, so a similar effect is present. At the same time, 
methods with few articles are not excluded from the top of 
the list. In the supervised classification example shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:recommendations-example-1-rec}, Gaussian 
Mixture Model appears relatively high despite having only 33 
articles, against several thousand for Random Forest, Support 
Vector Machine, and Neural Network. The same effect is visible in the 
anomaly detection example shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:recommendations-example-3-rec}, where Local 
Outlier Factor is ranked second despite having only 48 articles. 
This shows that a method with few articles can still rank high 
if those articles match the problem description closely.

Another aspect of the article-based recommendation is that 
MLguide uses article titles and abstracts as the text input for 
retrieval, not full article content. The motivation for this 
choice is described in 
Section~\ref{sec:article-retrieval-and-knowledge-extraction}: 
abstracts provide a consistent representation of each article's 
core content, while full texts include sections that make them 
less comparable across a corpus, and storing and matching around 
17000 full texts would also be costly. Abstracts are also more 
comparable in length to a problem description, which suits the 
same sentence embedding model. However, several works reviewed in 
Section~\ref{sec:semantic-retrieval-from-scientific-literature} 
point to the limitations of this approach. 
\textcite{park2026chain_of_retrieval} report that relying only 
on abstracts is suboptimal because abstracts offer only sparse, 
high-level summaries of a paper, and 
\textcite{ostendorff2022_aspect_based_embeddings} find that 
embeddings of titles and abstracts capture the task and dataset of a 
paper well but its method poorly, because methodological detail 
is mentioned only marginally in titles and abstracts.

The summary character of abstracts has visible consequences for 
MLguide. An abstract can list several methods used in a study, 
for example mentioning that one was the best while others were 
tested as baselines, but the method retrieval does not 
distinguish between them. All mentioned methods are treated as 
relevant to the problem the article addresses. The same applies 
when a method is used as part of a pipeline rather than as the 
main contribution, such as a dimensionality reduction step 
applied before the main method. Principal Component Analysis, 
described in Section~\ref{sec:machine-learning-methods} as a 
dimensionality reduction method, appears among the 
recommendations in the supervised classification example 
(Section~\ref{sec:example-1}), likely for this reason. The same 
issue applies to the classification of articles into ML areas, 
which is based on keywords in titles and abstracts. An article 
that presents an unsupervised approach while contrasting it 
with a supervised baseline can match both ML areas. As a 
result, methods that are mentioned but not recommended by the 
original authors, that are used as preprocessing steps, or 
that belong to a different ML area than the article's main 
contribution, can still appear in the recommendations.

A further challenge in extracting method names from text is 
handling cases where one method name is contained in another, 
such as Neural Network within Recurrent Neural Network. Each 
such case has to be handled explicitly. The article retrieval 
pipeline described in 
Section~\ref{sec:article-retrieval-and-knowledge-extraction} 
handles this for Neural Network, but the results suggest that 
not all relevant cases are covered. In the anomaly detection 
example, Support Vector Machine is at the top of the 
recommendations, while One-Class SVM appears further down. 
One-Class SVM is described in 
Section~\ref{sec:machine-learning-methods} as an anomaly 
detection method, and the top-ranked articles for Support 
Vector Machine in this example include a paper that explicitly 
concerns a one-class model. This indicates that articles about 
One-Class SVM contribute to the article count of both methods, 
but Support Vector Machine benefits from its much larger 
overall article count and ranks higher as a result.

A related point is that literature-based recommendations 
reflect the methods that appear in published research, which 
may differ from what is commonly used in practice or taught in 
ML courses. In the user testing for the anomaly detection problem in 
Project 1, several groups reported that neural network methods were 
over-represented in the recommendations, and that Gaussian 
Mixture Model and One-Class SVM were missing 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-1}). These are classical methods for 
anomaly detection specifically, while neural network methods 
have a broader range of applications, as described in 
Section~\ref{sec:neural-networks-and-deep-learning}. This 
helps explain why neural network methods have higher article 
counts in the database overall. 
Another possible factor is that research literature tends to use more 
advanced models than those that are typical starting points 
for users new to machine learning, so recommendations grounded 
in research can differ from what students or beginners would 
expect based on their course material.
The supervised classification 
example in Section~\ref{sec:example-1} shows a different 
balance, where classical methods such as Linear Regression 
and K-Nearest Neighbors also have high article counts. 
Classical methods are therefore not under-represented as a 
whole, but they can be under-represented within specific tasks 
or areas. 

In the supervised classification example 
(Section~\ref{sec:example-1}), Gaussian Mixture Model is 
ranked second despite being flagged as not suited for the 
classification task. A likely reason is that the wording of 
the problem description, which includes terms such as 
"faulty", resembles how an unsupervised anomaly detection 
problem might be described. The article-based score for Gaussian Mixture Model is 
therefore high enough to rank it as the second recommendation. 
This shows that placing strong 
weight on the problem description, without filtering on attributes 
such as selected task in the ranking itself, can lead to recommendations 
that are clearly inconsistent with the user's stated intent.

The ontology in MLguide structures the method knowledge that 
complements the unstructured knowledge spread across the article 
database. It supports the filters in the user interface, gives 
transparency in how the recommendations are presented, and is 
designed to be extended over time. The hierarchy lets related 
methods share properties through inheritance, as described in 
Section~\ref{sec:ml-and-ontology}, which makes it easier to add 
new methods without specifying every property from scratch. 
This makes the ontology more flexible than a flat matrix as used 
by \textcite{riesener2020} and 
\textcite{lickert2021_ml_selection_reverse_logistics}, and more 
formal than the semantic wiki of 
\textcite{gerschutz2021semantic_web_approach}. It also gives 
the kind of transparency that AutoML systems are often 
criticised for lacking 
\parencite{barbudo2023eight_years_automl_categorisation_review_trends}. 
The ontology-based approach is comparable to AI4PD 
\parencite{gerschutz2023_AI4DP}, which also uses an
ontology to support method selection.

The usefulness of the ontology depends on how completely its 
properties are populated. In Project 1, several groups reported 
that the performance preferences were not useful in practice 
and that the task match was incorrect for some methods 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-1}). This was system version A, and 
the findings led to a temporary removal of these filters in 
version B while the method knowledge was being extended, as 
described in Section~\ref{sec:changes-across-system-versions}. 
Version C, used in the example-based evaluation, has higher 
property coverage. Most methods now have task and dataset type relations, 
and many have performance preferences defined, while the note properties, 
such as conditions for when a method is possible or not 
recommended, are still sparsely populated. The overall coverage 
is not complete, as shown in 
Table~\ref{tab:ml-approach-coverage-extended}.

The incomplete coverage reflects a wider challenge with 
ontology-based method knowledge. Populating the properties 
manually is time-consuming, and it is difficult to keep up with 
a growing set of methods and tasks. Related work acknowledges 
this. \textcite{gerschutz2023_AI4DP} report that populating 
their ontology with method knowledge is done manually and that 
the effort required is high. \textcite{sonntag2023pattern_ml_product_development} 
and \textcite{lickert2021_ml_selection_reverse_logistics} 
restrict their work to a much smaller number of methods, which keeps 
the effort manageable but limits the scope. MLguide aims to 
cover a broader range of methods across the full product 
lifecycle, which is more ambitious in scope but exposes the 
manual-effort limitation more clearly.

Some structural choices in the ontology are also worth 
discussing. The dataset type of a method is not stored as a 
property on the method itself, but is reached through the 
\texttt{used\_for} relation to a task, as explained in 
Section~\ref{sec:ml-ontology-structure-and-content}. This means 
a method only matches a dataset type if it is connected to a 
task that has that dataset type defined. One result is that the 
same information can be expressed in two ways. A task such as 
"Tabular Regression" overlaps with the combination of the task
"Regression" and a tabular dataset type. Another result is that 
some matches can be missing in unexpected places. In the 
supervised classification example 
(Section~\ref{sec:example-1}), Principal Component Analysis is 
not marked as matching the tabular dataset type. This follows 
from the fact that the tasks PCA is linked to in the ontology 
do not have tabular as a defined dataset type. A cleaner 
separation, where dataset type is its own property of the 
method, would avoid such cases and make the ontology easier to 
query and reason over.

The structure is more flexible in how methods relate to ML 
areas. As shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:ontology-uml-with-extension}, methods are not 
linked directly to ML areas. Instead, they are linked to tasks 
through \texttt{used\_for}, and tasks belong to ML areas. A 
method is also linked to the articles that mention it, and 
each article is classified by ML area, so a method can be 
associated with multiple areas through both tasks and 
articles. A method that does not fit neatly into the three-way 
division of supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement 
learning can therefore still be part of the ontology and 
appear in recommendations. Example 2 
(Section~\ref{sec:example-2}) illustrates this. Two 
metaheuristic methods appear in the recommendations for a 
reinforcement learning problem, Genetic Algorithm at third 
place and Particle Swarm Optimization further down. Neither 
is a reinforcement learning method, but the retrieved 
articles connect them to similar scheduling problems. This is 
consistent with the literature, where evolutionary algorithms 
are often combined with the three common ML areas 
(Section~\ref{sec:machine-learning-paradigms-and-tasks}). The 
ontology accommodates this without forcing a classification 
that would not be a good fit.

The ML Ontology developed by \textcite{humm2026industry_ready_machine_learning_ontology} 
was originally built to support AutoML rather 
than method selection. As a result, several of its classes are 
not used in MLguide, and a number of extensions had to be added 
to support the article-method links and the filters that 
MLguide depends on. Building on an existing ontology gives 
MLguide a stable foundation and aligns it with a broader effort 
in the field, but it also means that the structure was not 
designed for the exact purpose at hand. The design nevertheless leaves room for MLguide to be 
extended with AutoML knowledge in the future, similar to 
how OMA-ML 
\parencite{zender2022ontology_based_meta_automl} builds on 
the same ML ontology to support AutoML 
(Section~\ref{sec:tools-and-platforms}).

The link between the ontology and the articles is one of the 
strengths of MLguide compared with the structured frameworks presented
in Section~\ref{sec:approaches-to-method-selection}, which rely 
entirely on manually populated method knowledge. In MLguide, 
each article is automatically classified by ML area and linked 
to the methods it mentions, as part of the article retrieval 
pipeline 
(Section~\ref{sec:article-retrieval-and-knowledge-extraction}). 
This means the article-method connection grows as the article 
base grows, without manual effort for each method. 
Other ontology-based work reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related-work-and-existing-tools} 
also makes use of links from the 
ontology to articles. NLP-KG \parencite{schopf2024nlp} links 
publications to fields of study and uses the graph for 
exploratory search, while ITO 
\parencite{blagec2022_benchmark_knowledge_graph} imports 
articles automatically from Papers with Code and links them 
to benchmark results for provenance. These approaches show 
that more can be extracted from the literature than what 
MLguide currently uses, and that automated import can reduce 
the manual effort required to keep an ontology up to date.

A natural direction for further work is therefore to extend 
the article retrieval pipeline to bring more structured 
knowledge into the ontology automatically. Possible additions 
include benchmark results, task or application area 
information, and combinations of methods used together in a 
pipeline or hybrid architecture. 
The last point connects to two observations made earlier. 
Section~\ref{sec:method-recommendations-and-role-of-articles} 
discussed how methods used in a pipeline, such as 
dimensionality reduction before a main method, can appear in 
the recommendations because the retrieval does not distinguish 
between them. Project 2 reports also noted that MLguide is 
limited to single-algorithm recommendations and does not 
capture hybrid architectures 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-2}). Extracting method combinations 
from the literature could turn this limitation into a feature, 
by treating pipelines and hybrid architectures as recommendable 
units in themselves. These extensions 
would reduce the manual effort required to keep the ontology 
up to date even more, and build on the article-ontology link that MLguide 
already has.

\section{Production Domain Knowledge}
\label{sec:production-domain-knowledge}

The previous section discussed how the article retrieval 
pipeline could be extended to bring more structured knowledge 
into the ontology automatically. One area where this would 
matter is the representation of production domain knowledge in 
the recommendations, which is the focus of this section.

The ontology in MLguide represents general machine learning 
knowledge, organised around ML areas, tasks, dataset types, 
and performance preferences. It does not represent production domain knowledge, 
that is, the kinds of problems found in product development and 
production, such as predictive maintenance, quality 
prediction, or fault diagnosis 
(Section~\ref{sec:applications-of-machine-learning}). The two 
dimensions from the pre-master project that would have brought 
such knowledge into the system, product lifecycle phase and 
application area, are not used in MLguide. As described in 
Section~\ref{sec:adaptations-for-mlguide}, both were dropped 
because the classification of articles along these dimensions 
was not reliable enough to support filtering without risking 
the exclusion of relevant articles.

The consequence is that the production-domain orientation of 
the recommendations comes only from the article retrieval, 
not from structured knowledge. The semantic similarity between 
the user's problem description and the article abstracts 
pulls in articles that are topically related to the problem, 
including its application context, even though no explicit 
representation links methods to production domains. This works 
in part, but the system has no way of guaranteeing that a 
recommendation is appropriate for a specific application area.

The frameworks reviewed in 
Section~\ref{sec:approaches-to-method-selection} take the 
opposite approach to scope. 
\textcite{lickert2021_ml_selection_reverse_logistics} works 
within reverse logistics, and 
\textcite{sonntag2023pattern_ml_product_development} focuses 
on early product development. The narrow scope makes it 
feasible to characterise methods against a relevant set of 
problems for that domain, but limits how widely the framework 
can be applied. MLguide makes the opposite trade-off: broad 
coverage across the full product lifecycle, but no domain 
knowledge built into the ontology yet. Ideally, the ontology 
would link methods to domain-specific problems and the 
articles would be filtered or weighted by application area, 
combining the strengths of both approaches.

The reason this is left as future work is again the manual 
effort. Characterising every method against a set of 
production-domain problems is a substantial task, and the 
article classification would have to be accurate enough to 
support filtering. If domain knowledge is added but left 
incomplete, partial coverage may give worse results than no 
filter at all, because methods that should match a domain 
would appear unmarked.

Without explicit filters for the application context, the way 
the user describes the problem becomes the main signal for what 
the system should retrieve. MLguide uses free-text problem 
descriptions as the main input, supported by an optional filter 
for ML area.
The pattern language of 
\textcite{sonntag2023pattern_ml_product_development}, by 
contrast, requires the user to formalise the entire problem 
in a fixed set of attributes before matching. In their user 
study the correct method was identified in \SI{64}{\percent} of 
cases, and the errors arose mainly from incorrect manual 
formalisation rather than from the matching itself. This 
suggests that requiring users to fill in fixed attributes to 
scope their problem can introduce errors. The free-text 
approach in MLguide keeps the burden of formalisation away 
from the user and lets the system interpret the description 
through semantic similarity. The anomaly detection example in 
Section~\ref{sec:example-3} shows that this can work without 
the ML area filter applied. With only a free-text problem 
description and a task selection, the top recommendations 
were still relevant anomaly detection methods. This indicates 
that the system is feasible without multiple filters
for scoping the problem.

An interesting and potentially positive effect of not having production 
domain knowledge in the ontology 
is that MLguide is relatively easy to adapt to a different 
domain. The article base can be replaced with articles from 
another field, and the recommendations will reflect that 
field through the semantic retrieval. This flexibility comes 
from the same design choice that limits the domain-specific 
precision. Adding deeper production domain knowledge would therefore be a trade-off: 
it could improve precision within product development and production, 
but would make the system less flexible and portable.

\section{Starter Notebooks and Implementation Support}
\label{sec:starter-notebooks-and-implementation-support}

Starter notebooks are one of three output types in MLguide. 
They give the user a concrete starting point for implementing 
a chosen method. Two groups in Project 1 reported code 
templates and implementation hints as a benefit 
(Section~\ref{sec:project-1}). The evidence base is limited, 
but the observation indicates that the output type has a place 
in how the system is used.

A notebook is positioned as a starting point for the user, not 
a replacement for implementation. This is different from 
AutoML systems, which produce a trained model directly and 
is known to treat the selection and configuration process as a black box 
(Section~\ref{sec:approaches-to-method-selection}). MLguide 
leaves the implementation to the user but provides a 
structured starting point that lowers the barrier to begin. 
This is also a more direct combination of method selection and 
implementation support than what the reviewed work offers. The 
frameworks for method selection in 
Section~\ref{sec:approaches-to-method-selection} stop at the 
method recommendation. Traingenerator 
\parencite{rieke2021traingenerator} generates code templates 
but assumes that the method has already been chosen. MLguide 
combines the two: a recommended method and a notebook that 
gives a starting point for implementing it.

The current templates are simple, and the coverage is 
incomplete. Adding 
a template for each method is manual work, similar to the 
manual effort that limits the ontology coverage. However, the 
technical structure is straightforward, and a new 
template only needs to be placed in the right folder with the right
method file name
to be picked up by the system, which makes it easy to extend over 
time.

Reinforcement learning methods are not covered by any 
templates, as noted in 
Section~\ref{sec:template-structure-and-extensibility}. This 
showed in Example 2, where Q-Learning was recommended but no 
template was available (Section~\ref{sec:example-2}). 
Supporting reinforcement learning would require a different 
approach. Options include providing templates for common 
environment patterns, or leaving the environment definition 
to the user while supplying a skeleton for the agent and the 
training loop. Investigating these options is left as future 
work.

The templates could also be developed further beyond what 
they contain today. The user could 
be given options to choose or adjust parameters through the 
interface before the notebook is generated, similar to the 
sidebar interface in Traingenerator described in 
Section~\ref{sec:tools-and-platforms}. However, parameter selection 
can be difficult for users new to machine learning, 
which is part of the target group for MLguide. A default 
configuration should therefore remain available so that the 
notebook works as a starting point even without further 
input from the user. The underlying ontology already supports 
this kind of extension. The \texttt{configuration\_item} 
class from the ML Ontology, shown in 
Section~\ref{sec:ml-ontology-structure-and-content}, gives a 
structure for storing parameters and their values. Filling 
this with reasonable defaults, possibly extracted from 
articles where parameter choices are reported, would let 
the system suggest defaults that go beyond a fixed template 
while remaining useful to users who do not want to choose 
themselves.

\section{Method Feedback}
\label{sec:method-feedback}

The method-specific feedback mechanism, given through the 
rating widget on the method details page 
(Section~\ref{sec:ui-method-details-page}), was introduced 
in version C and was therefore not part of the user testing 
in Project 1 or Project 2 
(Section~\ref{sec:changes-across-system-versions}). Few 
ratings have been submitted so far, and the mechanism could 
not be evaluated as a data source. This is the cold start 
problem discussed in 
Section~\ref{sec:user-feedback-in-recommendation-systems}: 
ratings only become useful when enough of them have been 
collected. The Bayesian smoothing in the feedback boost 
(Section~\ref{sec:feedback-boost}) is designed to handle a 
small user base by pulling the estimate towards a neutral 
prior when ratings are sparse. The combined ranking still 
relies mostly on the similarity-based score. This is 
a deliberate design choice for the current stage, but it 
also means that feedback is unlikely to influence the 
recommendations meaningfully until the user base grows.

Each rating is stored together with the full problem 
description and the rated method, as described in 
Section~\ref{sec:database-structure}. This structure 
supports content-based filtering 
\parencite[Chapter~4]{ricci2010recommender_systems_handbook}, 
where recommendations are based on similarity between items 
that the users have rated before. Since each rating 
in MLguide is tied to the problem description it was given 
for, the similarity between a user's current problem and 
problems that have been rated before could feed into the 
ranking, giving more weight to methods that have been rated 
highly for similar problems. In this way the system would 
collect knowledge from 
its users and use it directly in the recommendations, which 
would reduce some of the manual-effort limitations discussed 
in earlier sections by drawing on user input rather than only 
on updates to the data sources. This is left as future work.

\section{Evaluation Approach}
\label{sec:evaluation-approach}

The two evaluation methods used for MLguide are complementary, 
but each has limitations that shape how the results should be 
read.

The user testing was carried out with engineering students 
in a machine learning course, which matches the intended 
user group of MLguide described in 
Section~\ref{sec:design-goals}: engineers and students new 
to machine learning. The two course projects had different 
topics and gave the students real implementation tasks to 
carry out (Section~\ref{sec:user-testing}). The students 
also chose their own specific problems within the project 
briefs, so the system was tested on a range of different 
problems rather than on repeated variations of the same one.

The user testing also has some constraints worth noting. 
The two projects used different system versions, 
as shown in the development timeline in 
Section~\ref{sec:development-timeline}. This reflects the 
iterative nature of the development, but it means that 
findings from Project 1 and Project 2 are not directly 
comparable. Neither project tested the final version C, which 
was the version used in the example-based evaluation. 
The number of groups using MLguide was also lower in Project 
2 than in Project 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:participation-count-p2}). 
Project 2 had a more open-ended task brief, while Project 1 
asked the groups to explore a more defined problem. The 
defined-problem setup matches better with the recommendation 
process MLguide is designed around, where the user has a 
specific problem and looks for a method. This may explain 
why Project 1 generated more direct feedback on the method 
recommendations, while Project 2 generated more feedback on 
the article retrieval.

None of the groups used the structured feedback 
form available in the system, so all feedback came as free text in the 
project reports without a fixed format. Interpreting the 
reports involved subjective judgement on the part of the 
author. A likely reason the feedback form was not used is 
that it was an optional feature in MLguide, not a required 
part of the course deliverable.

The groups were also free to use MLguide at any point in 
their project. In Project 1, about half the groups used the 
system early, before choosing methods, and about half used 
it after the methods had already been chosen 
(Figure~\ref{fig:timing-p1}). The overlap between 
recommendations and used methods was similar for both groups 
(Figure~\ref{fig:overlap-by-timing-p1}), but the overlap 
means different things in the two cases. For groups that 
used MLguide early, the overlap indicates whether the system 
influenced their choice of methods. For groups that used it 
late, the overlap only indicates whether the recommendations 
match methods that had already been chosen on a different 
basis, likely from course material or online resources. 
Several groups recognised this themselves, and the most 
commonly reported issue in Project 1 was that MLguide was 
used too late to influence method decisions 
(Figure~\ref{fig:issues-p1}).

The free use of the system also highlights an unintended 
use case. Several groups that used MLguide late reported 
that they used it to confirm or validate methods they had 
already chosen (Figure~\ref{fig:benefits-p1}). MLguide 
therefore appears to serve two roles, helping users choose 
methods when used early, and providing a check on methods 
chosen elsewhere when used late. The second was not an 
intended function of the system, but it was reported as 
useful by several groups.

The example-based evaluation complements the user testing by 
offering controlled input. This made it possible to examine 
the recommendation behaviour in detail and to detect 
patterns that would have been difficult to identify from open use 
alone, such as the high occurrence of methods with high article counts.
The trade-off is that the examples were constructed 
by the author, and the output was interpreted by the author 
as well. This introduces a risk of bias in both how the 
problem descriptions were phrased and how the results were 
read. The number of examples is also small and covers only a 
limited part of the range of problems MLguide could be 
applied to.

The two evaluations together give a reasonable basis for 
discussing how the system behaves and where its limitations 
lie, but the findings should be understood within the scope 
of this evaluation. A larger and more independent 
evaluation, including industry users and a broader set of 
examples, would give stronger conclusions.
